# DRAFT — the income lessons (English only, for approval)

**Status:** not wired into the app. Deliberately outside `src/content/`, because
`check-data.mjs` enforces 5-language parity on lesson modules and this is English-only
until approved. Nothing here ships as-is.

**Proposed placement:** `track: "money"` (the judgment track), ids **41–44**, inserted at the
**start** of the money block in `lessons.js`'s array — `lessonsInTrack` preserves array
position, so these open the track ahead of lesson 16 rather than trailing it. That matters:
they are the frame the other thirteen judgment lessons hang on.

**Two constraints held throughout, both from this repo's closed blindspots:**

1. **§10.1 — no advice.** Every income type is described by how it *behaves* (does it continue
   when you stop, what it demands up front, what can go wrong), never by what a reader should
   go and earn. There is no "you should", no "best", no "start a business".
2. **§10.2 shape — no guru branding.** The ideas here are the common property of most books
   written about money. The app does not name or brand itself with any of them, exactly as
   the Dalio branding was removed. Lesson 16 already uses the right dodge: *"a name that turns
   up in nearly every book written about money."*

**On the ordering you gave** (`labor < passive <= business <= investment`): it is carried, but
as a *mechanism* rather than a verdict — the spectrum is how tightly each kind is coupled to
your hours, and how much of it can keep running without you. Lesson 44 is the deliberate
counterweight, because "passive income" taught without its entry cost is the single most
misleading idea in this whole area.

## Lesson 41 — "The Subject That Wasn't on the Timetable"

- **icon:** 🎓 · **minutes:** derived by `check-data.mjs` §2, do not hand-set
- **subtitle:** You were taught to earn money, not to understand it

### Section 1 — Thirteen Years, and Not One Hour on This

Think about everything school found time for. Photosynthesis. The water cycle. Quadratic
equations, for years. Whole terms on wars that ended centuries ago.

Now try to remember the lesson on what a payslip deduction actually was. Or how interest
worked on the loan you'd be offered at eighteen. Or what to do with the first money you ever
earned, beyond "save some of it."

For most people there wasn't one. Personal finance appears in some school systems, patchily
and recently, and often as a single optional module. A great many working adults — including
people who did very well at school — reached their first payslip having never once been
taught how money behaves after it arrives.

It's worth being careful about why, because the honest answer isn't a conspiracy. Curricula
are built to be examinable and to prepare people for work, and "be good at a job" is a
coherent thing to optimize for. Money skills are local — tax rules, pension systems and
credit reporting differ by country and change every few years — which makes them awkward to
standardize and quick to go stale. Nobody sat in a room and decided you shouldn't know this.
It simply fell between the subjects.

But the effect is the same either way. You were trained, carefully and for over a decade, in
how to be hired. You were not trained in what happens to the money once you are.

### Section 2 — What Fills the Gap Instead

A gap like that doesn't stay empty. Something teaches you about money whether or not anyone
intended it to.

Usually it's your family, which means you inherit their assumptions along with their habits —
including the ones that didn't work. Some of what people "know" about money is really just
what they watched a parent do at close range for eighteen years. Then there's whoever is
paying to reach you: an ad, an app with a green arrow, a confident stranger on a screen whose
income comes from your attention rather than from their advice being right. And then there's
plain experience, which is a real teacher but an unusually slow and expensive one, because in
money the feedback often arrives years after the decision.

None of that is a substitute for having been taught. It's what happens when you weren't.

This is not a lesson about blame, and it isn't a claim that you're behind. It's the frame for
the ones that follow. If you have ever quietly assumed that everyone else got some briefing
you missed — they didn't. Most people are working from the same three sources above. The
rest of this track is an attempt to replace some of that with something you can actually
inspect, starting with the most basic question there is: where does money come from?

### Takeaway

Almost nobody is taught how money works, and the gap gets filled anyway — by family habit, by
whoever is paying for your attention, and by slow expensive experience. Not knowing this
already isn't a personal failing; it's the ordinary result of a subject that fell between the
others.

### Think About

Write down one thing you believe about money — about debt, saving, property, anything. Now
try to name where it came from. A parent, a teacher, an ad, something that happened to you?
Some of your beliefs will have a clear source and some won't, and the ones that don't are the
interesting ones.

### Check question

> **Q.** Why is personal finance largely absent from most school curricula?
>
> - a) There is nothing useful that could be taught about it
> - b) Curricula favour examinable subjects, and money rules are local and change often ✅
> - c) It is deliberately withheld to keep people in debt
> - d) Every country already teaches it thoroughly
>
> **explain:** Curricula are built around examinable, standardizable subjects, and money rules
> differ by country and change every few years — so the topic is awkward to standardize and
> goes stale quickly. It fell between subjects rather than being withheld on purpose. The
> effect on you is the same either way, which is why it's worth naming.

## Lesson 42 — "Four Ways Money Arrives"

- **icon:** 🧾 · **subtitle:** The same $1,000 can reach you in very different ways

### Section 1 — Same Amount, Four Different Origins

Four people each add about $1,000 to their month.

Priya works nine extra shifts at her hospital. Tom owns a small apartment and collects the rent on
it. Alina runs a two-person cleaning company; after she has paid her cleaner, her supplier and
herself for the hours she personally worked, about $1,000 is left over. Ben owns a parcel of
shares that pay a dividend, and this month the dividend lands.

Every one of them is $1,000 better off. If you looked only at their bank balances you could
not tell them apart. But the four thousand dollars arrived by four genuinely different
mechanisms, and the differences show up everywhere afterwards — in what happens next month, in
how each is taxed, in what each person had to own or build first, and in what could go wrong.

Roughly, the four are these. **Labour income** is money paid for your time and skill: wages,
salary, tips, freelance fees. **Investment income** is money produced by an asset you own:
dividends, interest, a gain when something you hold is sold for more than you paid. **Business
income** is what's left of a venture's revenue after its costs, when the venture is something
you own rather than something you're employed by. And **passive income** is the loose everyday
name for money that keeps arriving from something already built or already bought — rent on a
property, royalties on a book, license fees — without your hours being spent again each time.

### Section 2 — Why the Names Are Blurrier Than They Look

Those four categories are useful, and they are worth holding loosely, because real income keeps
refusing to sit in one box.

Tom's rent is called passive, but if the boiler fails he is on the phone at 7am, and if the
apartment sits empty the income is simply zero while the mortgage isn't. Alina's business income
looks like a fifth thing, but part of it is really payment for her own hours — she is partly an
employee of her own company — and only the remainder is genuinely the business earning rather
than her earning. Ben's dividend arrived with no effort at all this month, but somebody had to
save the money that bought those shares, which almost certainly came from labour income
originally.

That last point is worth sitting with, because it's the honest shape of the whole thing:
almost every pound of the other three started life as labour income belonging to someone. The
categories aren't a ladder people climb by escaping work. They describe what a pound is
*doing* now, not where it came from or how virtuous it is.

So the four names are a lens, not a taxonomy to be argued over. What actually distinguishes
them is a single question, and it turns out to be a much sharper tool than the labels
themselves. That question is the next lesson.

### Takeaway

The same amount of money can arrive as pay for your time, as the output of something you own,
as what's left over from a venture, or as rent on something already built — and the four
behave very differently afterwards. The categories blur constantly, and nearly all of the
other three began as somebody's labour income.

### Think About

Think of every source of money that reached you in the last year, including small and one-off
ones. Which of the four is each closest to? Most people find their list is almost entirely one
category, which is worth noticing without reading anything into it.

### Check question

> **Q.** Priya works extra shifts; Tom collects rent on an apartment he owns. Both receive about
> $1,000. What is the most accurate thing to say about the two?
>
> - a) Tom's $1,000 is worth more than Priya's
> - b) They are identical, since the amounts match
> - c) They arrived by different mechanisms, which differ in what they demand and what can go wrong ✅
> - d) Only Priya's counts as real income
>
> **explain:** The amounts are the same and neither is worth more per pound. What differs is
> the mechanism: Priya's is paid for hours she worked, Tom's is produced by an asset he had to
> buy first, and each carries its own demands and its own ways of failing — an empty apartment pays
> nothing, while a shift always pays.

## Lesson 43 — "Does It Stop When You Stop?"

- **icon:** ⏸️ · **subtitle:** One question separates the four more sharply than their names do

### Section 1 — The Question

Here is a test you can apply to any source of money in about five seconds.

*If you stopped working entirely for three months — illness, a new baby, burnout, a broken
leg — what would this income do?*

Run the four through it. Priya's shifts stop the day she does; her income goes to zero in
lockstep with her hours. Alina's business keeps trading if her cleaner keeps cleaning and her
customers keep calling, but shrinks in whatever proportion the business depended on her
personally — and for a two-person company that proportion is large. Tom's rent keeps arriving
so long as the tenant stays and the building stands. Ben's dividend doesn't notice. It has no
idea he's ill.

That's the real axis, and it explains the ordering people reach for when they rank these
things. Labour income is the most tightly coupled to your hours: it is the only one of the
four that reliably becomes zero when you stop. Business income is partly coupled, in a ratio
you can actually estimate. Rent and royalties are loosely coupled. Investment income is barely
coupled to your time at all.

Nothing here says one is better. A ranking by time-coupling is not a ranking by worth, and
"barely coupled to your time" is not the same as "reliable" — the next lesson is largely about
that. But if you have ever wondered why so much writing about money circles the same
hierarchy, this is the engine underneath it. It isn't a claim about virtue. It's a claim about
what happens to the money when you're not there.

### Section 2 — What the Question Is Actually For

The point of the test isn't to make you dissatisfied with a job. Most people's income is
almost entirely labour income, that is completely ordinary, and a salary has real properties
the others don't — it is predictable, it usually arrives whether or not the company had a good
month, and it requires no capital to start.

The point is that "all my income stops the day I stop" is a fact about your situation, and
most people have never once stated it plainly. It's the kind of thing that stays invisible
precisely because it's universal in your circle — if everyone you know is in the same
position, it doesn't read as a fact about your finances at all. It just reads as how life is.

Stating it changes what you notice. It's why the same person can find an emergency fund
boring in the abstract and urgent the moment they've said out loud that their income has a
single point of failure, and why the difference between assets and liabilities from lesson 16
stops being a tidy definition and starts being about which column your own money is going
into. The question doesn't tell you to do anything. It tells you where you're standing, and
almost every decision in the rest of this track reads differently depending on the answer.

### Takeaway

Ask of any income: if you stopped for three months, what would it do? Labour income goes to
zero, business income shrinks in proportion to how much it needed you, rent and royalties
mostly continue, investment income barely notices — and that time-coupling, not any ranking of
worth, is what people are really pointing at when they order these four.

### Think About

Apply the three-month test to your own income, honestly, and write down the number it would
fall to. Then notice your reaction to that number rather than trying to fix it. Whatever it
is, it's information you didn't have written down before.

### Check question

> **Q.** Alina owns a small cleaning company with one employee and works in it herself. If she
> stopped working for three months, what would most likely happen to her business income?
>
> - a) It would stay exactly the same, since she owns the business
> - b) It would fall to zero immediately, like a wage
> - c) It would shrink roughly in proportion to how much the business depended on her personally ✅
> - d) It would increase, because she stopped taking pay
>
> **explain:** Business income sits between a wage and a passive source. The parts that run
> without her — her employee's work, existing customers — carry on; the parts that were really
> her own labour stop. In a two-person company that personal share is large, so the income
> shrinks substantially without disappearing.

## Lesson 44 — "The Part the Word 'Passive' Leaves Out"

- **icon:** 🪜 · **subtitle:** Every income that doesn't need your hours needed something else first

### Section 1 — Nothing Starts Passive

"Passive income" is one of the most oversold phrases in the language, and the reason is that
the word describes only the last stage of the process.

Go back to Ben's dividend, the one that doesn't notice he's ill. For that to land, somebody
had to accumulate the money that bought the shares, which for most people means years of
labour income not spent. Tom's rent needed a deposit, a mortgage he is personally liable for,
and a purchase that could have been a bad one. Alina's business needed her to work far more
than a job's hours for a stretch, with no guarantee at the end and her own money at risk. A
royalty needs the book written first, and most books earn very little.

So the honest version of the spectrum has a second axis running the other way. As income gets
less coupled to your hours, it generally demands more of something else up front — capital,
or years, or specialised skill, or a tolerance for the whole thing not working. The reason
labour income is where nearly everyone starts is not that people haven't heard of the
alternatives. It's that labour income is the only one of the four you can begin with nothing
but yourself.

### Section 2 — And It Can Go Wrong in Ways a Wage Can't

There's a second thing the word hides, which is that decoupling income from your hours also
decouples it from your control.

A wage is unusually well protected. In most countries it comes with legal minimums, notice
periods and a queue position ahead of other creditors if the employer fails — and if you turn
up and do the work, you are paid, regardless of whether the business had a good quarter. None
of that protection follows you into the other three. A tenant can stop paying while the
mortgage doesn't stop. A dividend can be cut at a board meeting you don't attend. An
investment can fall and stay fallen for a decade, and "it usually recovers" is a statement
about averages, not about the particular decade you happen to need the money in. A business
can consume years and return nothing, which is the ordinary outcome rather than the unlucky
one.

Hold both halves at once and the picture stops being a ladder and becomes a set of trades.
Labour income: reliable, protected, capped by your hours, and it stops when you stop.
Everything else: not capped by your hours, not protected, and it requires something before it
gives anything back. Neither column is the smart one. They're different shapes, and which
shape suits a given person depends on things this app cannot know about you — which is exactly
why nothing in this track will tell you which to pursue.

What it will do is make sure you know the trade exists, and that the word "passive" is doing a
lot of quiet work in most places you'll see it.

### Takeaway

Income that doesn't need your hours needed something else first — capital, years, skill, or
risk — and it gives up the protections a wage carries: legal minimums, notice periods, and
getting paid whether or not the business had a good month. The four aren't a ladder from worse
to better. They're trades, and the word "passive" describes only the last stage.

### Think About

Pick any "passive income" idea you have seen advertised recently. Ignore the returns it claims
and write down two other things instead: what it needs before it pays anything, and what would
have to happen for it to pay nothing at all. Both answers are usually in the material, in
smaller type than the first number.

### Check question

> **Q.** What does the phrase "passive income" tend to leave out?
>
> - a) That it is always larger than a wage
> - b) The capital, time, skill or risk required up front — and that it lacks a wage's protections ✅
> - c) That it is available to anyone immediately at no cost
> - d) That it is taxed identically to wages everywhere
>
> **explain:** "Passive" describes only the final stage, once something is already built or
> bought. Getting there takes capital, years, skill or risk, and the result gives up
> protections a wage has — legal minimums, notice periods, and being paid regardless of how the
> business did. It isn't a better rung on a ladder; it's a different trade.
